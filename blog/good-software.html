What made good software good

There was a time where humans communicated with machines by means of a special language called coding. Coding was a series of instructions we gave machines to execute our intent. It was also a language we shared with other humans, in hope that they would expand, enrich, and improve on our initial work.

Despite the overinflated ego among way too many engineers (myself included), getting machines to do stuff ended up being far from the herculean jigsaw puzzle it seemed to be in the 90s. The real challenge when resources got cheap was designing useful software for people with a diverse set of ever expanding needs and wants.

“The most efficient piece of software will get re-written if no one understands how it works or how to work with it - the days of the lone coder hacking away in a closet are gone”.

That was what my programming teacher said to us in my first programming class. For the next 20 years, those words proved to be more instructive than any tech conferences I attended.

I can’t begin listing all the times I’ve seen software written by brilliant people that rarely anyone could understand or even want to understand.

This is what makes software very different from building, say a bridge, where good software was an almost direct function of how well we collaborated with our peers.

In this pre-AI era, good software was described with words like clean, simple, elegant, and modular. Really what I think we were saying was “I can easily understand how these pieces work and it makes me want to contribute.”

Making machines do the thing was almost a byproduct of that collaboration, not the other way around. Of course users don’t pay for collaboration, they pay for results - businesses tolerated fuzzy human rituals because they had to.

What will happen to software when we commoditize the entire software development practice and replace human engineers with bots? All I know is that before 2025, good software could only be achieved if a team worked together and understood what was being built.
